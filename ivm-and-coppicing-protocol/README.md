# IVM (Integrated Vegetation Management) & COPPICING PROTOCOL

## Integrated Vegetation Management (IVM) 4-Tier Risk Architecture

#### Level 1: LOW (Buffer & Groundcover)

* Core Definition: Low-impact native or naturalized cover species that provide beneficial soil binding, phytoremediation, or swale buffering. They pose minimal threat to capping liners, gas systems, or wildlife biosecurity.
* Biological Criteria: Non-invasive root systems (shallow rhizomes or fibrous roots); absence of bioaccumulating edible fruit; non-allelopathic (does not release biochemicals that hinder or alter the growth, survival, or reproduction of nearby plants).
* Operational Field Action: Selective hand-thinning, plant division, or structural pruning executed when biomass encroaches on active drainage capacity or visual sightlines.
* Action Trigger: Vegetative cover exceeds 30–40% of swale channel volume or encroaches within 5 ft of fence lines or engineered capping liners.

#### Level 2: MED (Phyto Engine)

* Operational Role & Core Definition: The Primary Phytoremediation Fleet. This tier comprises major canopy-forming trees and deep-taproot hydraulic pumps deployed to transpire massive groundwater volumes, build clean topsoil, and shade out understory weeds.
* Biological Criteria: Deep vertical taproots or high-volume lateral root systems operating above or outside capping liners; rapid above-ground biomass accumulation; high evapotranspiration (ET) rates; non-edible foliage and seeds.
* Management Needs & Field Actions: Active mechanical management. These species are intentionally established on site, but due to their size and vigorous growth, they require scheduled coppicing ("chop & drop"), pollarding, crown shaping, submergence cutting (for aquatic species), and root-barrier containment (60-mil HDPE).
* Action Trigger: Canopy shade obscures sightlines or monitoring sensors, plant height exceeds storm windthrow thresholds, aquatic stand density exceeds 25% of channel volume, or clump perimeters reach within 6 ft of liner anchor trenches.

#### Level 3: CRITICAL (Infrastructure Hazard)

* Operational Role & Core Definition: Select High-Risk Infrastructure Threats. Level 3 species are non-edible plants that actively breach engineered capping liners, trigger methane gas extraction flash fires, collapse perimeter security infrastructure, or produce contact allergens that disrupt field personnel. Unlike Level 2 species (which are managed via scheduled coppicing and crown shaping), Level 3 species require targeted biological containment, mechanical extraction, or vegetative suppression before reaching structural maturity.
* Biological Criteria:
* Mechanical Capping Threats: Deep, lignified woody taproots or aggressive lateral root systems capable of penetrating sub-base marl seals, breaching geotextile capping membranes, or fracturing drainage structures.
* Thermal / Methane Vectors: High-density dry biomass production generating critical fuel loads adjacent to active landfill gas (LFG) extraction wellheads, flare stations, and header pipework.
* Structural & Spatial Interference: Heavy smothering vines or subterranean rhizomatous mats that invade liner anchor seams, compromise sub-grade drainage channels, or exert mechanical loads on perimeter security barriers and sensor arrays.
* Workforce Dermatological & Respiratory Hazards: Allelopathic species or plants producing airborne pollen and contact dermatoxins (Parthenium) that present direct occupational health hazards to field crews.
* General Technical Control Mechanics (Scientist Baseline):
* Phenological Extraction: Mechanical disturbance or uprooting timed strictly to pre-anthesis, pre-seed-set, or pre-coning windows to exhaust soil seed-bank persistence and halt vegetative tillering.
* Root-System Neutralization: Structural crown isolation, root-collar girdling, or light-deprivation stump capping executed to prevent epicormic resprouting without disturbing underlying geotextile or marl capping seals.
* Biomass Sub-Surface Depletion: Mechanical scalping, repeated stem severing, or high-temperature UV-polyethylene solarization applied to exhaust subterranean rhizome and tuber energy reserves over multi-week cycles.
* Action Trigger Framework (Scientist & Manager Baseline):
* Phenological Stage: Detection of target species prior to flower head anthesis, seed-pod maturation, cone formation, or fruit development.
* Physical Architecture Thresholds: Emergence reaching specified height or root-collar caliper limits (e.g., woody stems prior to taproot anchoring at <20–36 in; invasive vines reaching <5 ft in height on structural barriers; cespitose grass tussocks exceeding <6–24 in canopy height).
* Infrastructure Proximity Boundaries: Encroachment within defined safety offsets around critical assets (e.g., flammable biomass within 25–30 ft of methane gas wellheads or pipelines; creeping rhizomes or taproots within 15–50 ft of capping liner boundaries and perimeter ditches).

#### Level 4: CRITICAL NOT SAFE (Biohazard / Unsafe)

* Core Definition: High-hazard species that produce edible fruits, seeds, or berries over chemical or leachate sectors, OR hemiepiphytic/buttressed trees that cause catastrophic structural and cap destruction.
* Biological Criteria: Production of edible biomass that bioaccumulates heavy metals and hydrocarbons into local fauna (birds, bats, iguanas, and humans); OR production of destructive aerial drop-roots and buttress plates that fracture marl caps and masonry structures.
* Operational Field Action: Absolute Zero Tolerance & Strict Biosecurity Containment. Execution of flush-cutting, bark-flare girdling, double-bagging of seed and fruit pods, UV-stabilized solarization, and light-deprivation stump capping.
* Action Trigger: Immediate containment and extraction upon emergence at the cotyledon or seedling stage.

#### **The National Healing Sanctuary (NHS) strictly prohibits the use of systemic, toxic herbicides (such as Triclopyr or Glyphosate) for site management. Instead, the NHS utilizes an advanced mechanical protocol known as Coppicing. By routinely cutting target vegetation back to the stump, we keep the subterranean root systems alive to act as biological rebar—locking toxic soils in place and preventing erosion—while permanently preventing the plants from reaching maturity. This ensures zero wind-resistance during hurricanes and guarantees that no flowers, fruits, or seeds are ever produced, completely neutralizing the risk of heavy metals entering the local food chain.**&#x20;

### Detailed Protocol: The "Chop & Drop" Biosecurity Method

1\. The Biological Anchor (Hurricane & Erosion Control) Unlined landfill slopes face severe erosion risks during the rainy season and catastrophic wind-shear risks during tropical cyclones. Allowing deep-taproot trees to grow a full canopy creates a "sail effect," where high winds can uproot the tree and expose toxic subterranean sectors.

* The NHS Solution: We preserve the living root mass underground to stabilize the soil, but mechanically remove the top-growth. The living roots hold the slope together, but without a canopy, the wind passes harmlessly over the sector.

2\. Absolute Biosecurity (The Zero-Fruiting Mandate) Unmonitored plant growth on a chemical site inevitably leads to the production of wild fruits, seeds, and root vegetables which are consumed by local wildlife or unknowingly harvested by humans.

* The NHS Solution: Target species are maintained in a perpetual "juvenile vegetative state." Because the top-growth (whips and shoots) is routinely slashed back to the stump before the plant reaches sexual maturity, the site is rendered biologically incapable of producing fruit. Heavy metals remain locked underground in the roots, and the above-ground food chain remains entirely uncontaminated.

3\. Mechanical Execution (Non-Toxic Top-Growth Management) To maintain the subterranean grid without introducing secondary chemical contamination to the groundwater, site practitioners will follow mechanical-only IVM guidelines:

* Routine Slashing: Green shoots and suckers are allowed to grow just enough to act as solar panels to keep the root mass alive. Once or twice a year, these shoots are mechanically slashed back to the stump.
* Chop and Drop Biomass: Slashed green material (having been tested to confirm it is free of heavy-metal translocation) is left directly on the ground. As this clean organic matter decomposes, it rapidly builds a safe, healthy layer of topsoil over the hazard zone, accelerating the phytostabilization process.
* Stump Desiccation (If Eradication is Required): If a specific stump must be permanently neutralized without toxic herbicides, practitioners will utilize light-deprivation (heavy UV-blocking tarps) or localized Epsom salt desiccation to safely rot the root mass in place.
